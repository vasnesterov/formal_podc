Best-Response Mechanisms — PODC 2011

Noam Nisan, Michael Schapira, Gregory Valiant, Aviv Zohar. Brief announcement: Incentive-compatible distributed greedy protocols. PODC 2011. DOI 10.1145/1993806.1993871. Formalized version: the full version Best-Response Mechanisms (ICS 2011, 12 pp., author copy https://www.cs.yale.edu/homes/schapira/BRM.pdf); numbering follows it. The brief announcement has no proofs.

DOI · PDF · All differences · Lean source · GitHub

Verdict: 🔧 Easy fix. The main theorem (Theorem 2.7) holds with a different round bound. Best-response mechanisms for NBR-solvable games with a clear outcome converge within e_G rounds, but incentive compatibility needs more rounds than e_G. The bound of the paper's own informal theorem, Σᵢ|Sᵢ| − n, is enough. The outdated-information extension needs rounds counted by how fresh the information is. The applications hold: stable roommates (correlated markets, and intern–hospital markets once proposals are bipartite, as intern–hospital matching means), cost sharing and BGP (with its missing No-Dispute-Wheel hypothesis restored), and so does the incentive compatibility of their mechanisms. TCP games (Section 3.4) and Section 4 were not checked.

Mistakes in the paper

🔧 Theorem 2.7 (2) (incentive compatibility after e_G rounds)

With exactly e_G rounds the best-response mechanism need not be incentive compatible. In a 4-player game with binary strategies, strict utilities, e_G = 2 and a clear outcome, a player who never best-responds ends 2 rounds of round robin with utility 2 instead of 1 (Counterexamples.theorem_2_7_false).

Impact. Local: the theorem holds with max(e_G, tᵢ) rounds, where tᵢ is the step of the clear-outcome order at which i is first restricted. It also holds with the Σᵢ|Sᵢ| − n rounds of the informal theorem in §1.3. The mechanisms of Section 3 run n rounds, which is enough for all of them.

Details

The deviator i is confined to the subgame G_{tᵢ} of its player-specific elimination order only after tᵢ rounds, and tᵢ can exceed e_G. In the example, i has the dominant strategy 0. Player k plays 1 iff i = 1 and j₂ = 1, j₂ copies j₁, and j₁ has the dominant strategy 0. The fast elimination uses i's dominance to fix k in two steps. Player i's own clear-outcome order must first fix j₁, j₂ and k (tᵢ = 3). Started at (1,1,1,1) with order i, k, j₂, j₁, the deviation keeps k = 1 for two rounds, so (i,k) = (1,1) gives i utility 2. If Definition 2.4 is read as "one player per step", e_G = 4 there. A second game with a 6-step "ladder" defeats that reading as well, since the deviation still pays after 4 rounds (Counterexamples.theorem_2_7_false_single_steps).

Repair

theorem_2_7_incentive_compatible proves the theorem with ≥ max(r, tᵢ) rounds in every run. It covers adaptive activation orders and simultaneous moves. theorem_2_7_limsup gives the lim sup version. informal_theorem proves it with Σᵢ|Sᵢ| − n rounds, using ClearWitness.exists_short.

🔧 Theorem 2.7, extension (1) (outdated information)

With outdated information, "a round = every player is active at least once" does not give convergence. In a two-step game, every player can best-respond at every step to information from time 0, and the run never converges (Counterexamples.outdated_information_false).

Impact. Local: rounds must be counted by how fresh the information is. Then both convergence and the incentive-compatibility bound hold.

Repair

AsyncRounds: from the end of round k+1 on, every current strategy is a best response to information produced in round k+1 or later. Under it, converges proves Theorem 2.7 (1) and deviator_utility_le proves the core of Theorem 2.7 (2). Both allow simultaneous moves, outdated information and adaptive orders.

✏️ Slips

  • Theorem 3.6 omits the hypothesis "No Dispute Wheel". The proof and the text use it, and without it the theorem is false: BAD GADGET has no pure Nash equilibrium, so it is not NBR-solvable (BGP.badGadget_not_NBRSolvable). Connectivity of the network is also implicit.

  • Remark after Definition 2.3: "NBR elimination has a unique order-independent outcome, a PNE". This fails for infinite strategy spaces, which the paper allows. With one player, S = ℕ and u(x) = x, every x is a one-step outcome and none is an equilibrium (Counterexamples.nbr_outcome_not_unique). The remark holds when tie-broken best responses exist, as every best-response mechanism presupposes (Solves.unique, Solves.isNash).

  • §3.2 tie-breaking "if |s − vᵢ| ≤ |t − vᵢ| then t ≺ᵢ s" is reflexive. Under Definition 2.2 every bid is then NBR in every subgame (CostSharing.CostSharingRule.gameLit_isNBR, gameLit_solves). We repaired it to "strictly closer wins, and at equal distance the lower bid wins".

  • Theorem 3.1, intern–hospital matchings: the paper's game lets every student propose to every other student, so interns may rank interns. With three interns ranking each other cyclically and one hospital, the market is not cycle-free, not NBR-solvable, and has no stable matching (StableRoommates.internHospital_counterexample). This is an implied assumption: intern–hospital matching is bipartite, and the paper's cycle argument ("any cycle must include a hospital placed after a desired intern and before a less desired one") assumes it. With bipartite proposals the case is cycle-free and Theorem 3.1 holds (theorem_3_1_internHospital).

  • Theorem 3.1 "G's unique PNE": for an odd number of students the PNE is unique only under tie-breaking. A 3-student correlated market has two PNEs (StableRoommates.exists_two_nash).

🔧 Theorem 3.6 (BGP, finding a node that can guarantee its route)

"If no such node exists, a₀ can guarantee its most preferred route" is wrong as stated: unfixed nodes further down R₀ may still deviate.

Impact. Statement true (with No Dispute Wheel and connectivity). The last unfixed node on R₀ can guarantee its route (BGP.exists_guarantee, BGP.theorem_3_6).

🔧 Theorem 3.3 (e_G ≤ n)

The paper's elimination has 3 steps, so it gives e_G ≤ 3, which says nothing for n ≤ 2.

Impact. Statement true. The cases n = 1, 2 have separate sequences (CostSharing.CostSharingRule.elimParam_le_card).

🔧 Theorems 3.1/3.2 (clear outcome)

Theorem 3.1 neither states nor proves that the stable-roommates outcome is a clear outcome, which Theorem 3.2 needs.

Impact. Statement true. The same elimination sequence witnesses it (StableRoommates.theorem_3_1).

The formalizationmodel, Lean names of the results, trust

Model.

  • Game theory, no network model. The theorems are ex post, so we fix one realization of the private utilities: a normal-form game TieBreakGame ι S R with arbitrary strategy types, utilities in a linear order and tie-breaking relations (Podc/Foundations/GameTheory/NeverBestResponse.lean). Definitions 2.2–2.6 are literal. Subgames are products, and an elimination step removes NBR strategies of any set of players at once.

  • Dynamics, first form: arbitrary runs with AsyncRounds. This covers sequential, simultaneous, adaptive and outdated-information activations.

  • Dynamics, second form: a repeated-response mechanism (Mechanism) with a start profile and an activation rule that depends on the whole history. A player's repeated-game strategy maps histories to announcements. The best-response strategy announces br i (current profile) for a tie-broken best-response rule br.

  • Incentive compatibility means that repeated best response is a pure Nash equilibrium of the repeated game, against every history-dependent deviation. Total utility is the last-stage utility at a horizon (IsIncentiveCompatible), or the lim sup (theorem_2_7_limsup). No randomness.

  • The "pass" termination rule (extension 3) is checked statically, with no round bound needed: eq_of_all_pass, utility_le_of_others_pass.
