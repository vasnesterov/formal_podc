Binding Crusader Agreement — PODC 2022

Ittai Abraham, Naama Ben-David, Sravya Yandamuri. Efficient and Adaptively Secure Asynchronous Binary Agreement via Binding Crusader Agreement. PODC 2022. DOI 10.1145/3519270.3538426. Formalized version: the 11-page proceedings version (no arXiv version; the proofs it defers to the "full version" are supplied here).

Verdict: 🔧 Easy fix. All four (G)BCA protocols are correct as intended: Algorithm 3 (crash BCA, n ≥ 2t + 1), Algorithm 4 (Byzantine BCA, n ≥ 3t + 1, Lemmas 4.4–4.9), Algorithm 5 (crash GBCA) and Algorithm 6 (Byzantine GBCA) satisfy agreement, validity, binding and termination against an adaptive adversary, with at most 2/4/3/6 broadcasts per party. Two proof steps of Section 4 need local repairs, and the pseudocode of Algorithm 5 needs one more word. The deterministic core of the framework (Theorems 3.1, 3.3–3.5: agreement, validity, the binding step of termination) holds; the efficiency comparisons and the probabilistic parts of the round bounds were not checked.

Mistakes in the paper

✏️ Slips

  • Algorithm 5, line 9 (output "⊥ grade 2"). Read literally, "if all of the messages contain the same value v then decide v, 2" also fires when all n − t echo2's are ⊥, e.g. for n = 3, t = 1, inputs 0, 1, 0, no crash, so Algorithm 5 can output "⊥ grade 2", which is not a GBCA output. Line 10 counts ⊥ as "a value other than v", so "value" includes ⊥. In the example, every party first receives one ⟨val, 0⟩ and one ⟨val, 1⟩. Then everybody sends ⟨echo, ⊥⟩ and then ⟨echo2, ⊥⟩, and line 9 outputs ⊥ with grade 2. That is not one of the five buckets of Definition 3.2, so Theorem 5.1 fails for the literal code. Line 11 becomes dead code, and Algorithm 2, line 7 ("if grade = 2 then commit val") would commit ⊥. The prose above the algorithm says that a party with all ⊥ decides ⊥. We prove Theorem 5.1 for this intended version (all ⊥ gives ⊥ grade 0) (Alg5.decideG, Alg5.theorem_5_1). No other checked theorem or lemma is false.

Typos

  • Algorithm 6, line 11 reads "if 1 then"; it means "if (1) then", as in Algorithm 4, line 11.

Proof gaps in true statements (2)Lemma 4.5 · Lemma 4.9

🔧 Lemma 4.5 (validity of Algorithm 4)

The proof says that to decide 1 − v a non-faulty party "must have 1 − v in its approvedVals set (line 15)", but line 15 only asks for n − t messages ⟨echo3, 1 − v⟩.

Impact. Statement true; repaired by one more link of the chain (Alg4.validity).

Repair

An uncorrupted sender of ⟨echo3, 1 − v⟩ saw n − t messages ⟨echo2, 1 − v⟩. One of their senders is uncorrupted and has 1 − v approved, which needs an uncorrupted party with input 1 − v.

🔧 Lemma 4.9 (binding of Algorithm 4, adaptive adversary)

The proof argues with "the t + 1 echo3 messages from non-faulty parties", but under adaptive corruption who is non-faulty depends on the extension, while b must be fixed at the first decision.

Impact. Statement true; repaired by fixing b from the prefix (Alg4.binding, Alg6.graded_binding).

Repair

If some party has broadcast ⟨echo3, u⟩ with u ≠ ⊥, then b = u, by Lemma 4.8 applied inside the extension. Otherwise the decider's quorum contains at least n − t − |bad(τ)| genuine ⊥'s. A later ⟨echo3, w⟩ quorum would then need n − t parties among at most 2t candidates. The same repair works for Algorithm 6 through its first ⟨echo4, ⊥⟩.

The formalizationmodel, Lean names of the results, trust

Model.

  • Asynchronous message passing among n parties, with authenticated reliable channels and arbitrary finite delays. It is an interleaving system: a broadcast is one event, and each delivery is a separate event chosen by the adversary.

  • The adversary is adaptive. It corrupts up to t parties at any time. A corrupted party stops following the protocol. In the Byzantine mode it can make anybody receive anything from it (equivocation); in the crash mode it just stops, and its earlier broadcasts may be delivered to only some parties.

  • Liveness is proved for every weakly fair infinite execution. Messages between never-corrupted parties are delivered, and their enabled guards are eventually taken. Nothing is required of corrupted parties or injected messages. A round-robin execution shows that fair executions exist (exists_fair).

  • Protocol guards are liberal: "n − t messages, all with v" is "v from n − t parties", and either branch of a "wait until" may fire when both hold. This is a superset of the paper's behaviours, so safety transfers. Parties keep running after deciding.

  • Deterministic protocols; randomness appears only in the framework, as coin values per round. A strong coin is one value per round, a weak coin one value per party. The expected-round bound for Algorithm 1 is a counting statement over coin prefixes, for a deterministic adversary whose binding values depend on earlier coins only.

  • Safety statements are stronger than the paper's. Agreement, validity and binding cover all recorded decisions, also of parties corrupted after deciding, in every reachable state and every extension.

Proofs we supplied. Theorems 4.1, 5.1, 5.3 (Algorithms 3, 5, 6) are stated with proofs deferred to the full version. We prove them. For Algorithm 6, binding must be argued at the echo4 level. A grade-≥1 decision on v needs a genuine ⟨echo4, v⟩, which is why line 25 checks t + 1 echo4's. (Alg3.theorem_4_1, Alg5.theorem_5_1, Alg6.theorem_5_3)

"Communication rounds" = broadcasts, not asynchronous time. "Terminates within 4 communication rounds" (Lemma 4.7) is proved by counting messages, and that is what we verify (≤ 4 broadcasts per party).

In the normalized-delay measure, Algorithm 4 can take almost 5 time units. With n = 7, t = 2, the Byzantine parties make one correct party approve the minority value first. Then nobody gets n − t equal echo2's, and everybody waits until both values are approved at time ≈ 4. We checked this by an event simulation, not in Lean (scripts/latency.py).
