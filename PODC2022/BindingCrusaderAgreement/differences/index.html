Differences between the formalization and the paper

Rendered from differencies.md of Binding Crusader Agreement — PODC 2022.

Paper: Abraham, Ben-David, Yandamuri, Efficient and Adaptively Secure Asynchronous Binary Agreement via Binding Crusader Agreement, PODC 2022 (11-page proceedings version, the only one used; the "full version" it refers to was not available to us).

Statement-level observations

  1. ✏️ Algorithm 5, line 9 (all echo2 messages ⊥). Read literally, "if all of the messages contain the same value v then decide v, 2" also fires when all n − t messages carry ⊥: line 10 treats ⊥ as "a value other than v", so "value" includes ⊥ in this pseudocode. Example: n = 3, t = 1, inputs 0, 1, 0, no crash, every party first receives one ⟨val, 0⟩ and one ⟨val, 1⟩. Everybody sends ⟨echo, ⊥⟩, then ⟨echo2, ⊥⟩, and line 9 decides "⊥ grade 2". This is not one of the five buckets of Definition 3.2, so the literal algorithm does not implement GBCA (Theorem 5.1). Line 11 becomes dead code, and Algorithm 2, line 7 ("if grade = 2 then commit val") would commit ⊥. The prose just above the algorithm says that the party then decides ⊥. We model that: all ⊥ gives ⊥ grade 0 (Alg5.decideG), and prove Theorem 5.1 for this version. This is a typo with evident intent: it is recoverable from the prose, and the fix is "non-⊥" in line 9. It is the only statement-level issue. One could read "⊥ grade 2" as the ⊥ bucket and call this notation, but the literal output also breaks Algorithm 2. The same phrase in Algorithm 3, line 6 is harmless (⊥ is a valid BCA output).

  2. "Terminates in at most 4 (6, 3, 2) communication rounds" (Theorem 4.3 / Lemma 4.7, Theorem 5.3, Theorem 5.1, Theorem 4.1). Lemma 4.7's proof counts messages: "a party sends at most 4 messages". §3 fixes the measure as broadcasts ("we measure broadcasts, i.e., the number of communication steps"). We prove exactly this: every party broadcasts at most 4 (6, 3, 2) messages (out_ncard_le_*). Open remark (unchecked): this is not the asynchronous latency in the usual normalized-delay measure (each message delay ≤ 1). For Algorithm 4 with n = 7, t = 2 there is a schedule in which every correct party sends 4 messages but decides only at time 5 − ε. Inputs: A1, A2 = 1, B1, B2, B3 = 0. Both Byzantine parties send ⟨echo, 1⟩ only to B2, at time 2 − ε. All other delays are 1. B2 then approves 1 just before 0 and sends ⟨echo2, 1⟩, while everybody else sends ⟨echo2, 0⟩. Now nobody can use the n − t same-echo2 branch. Everybody has to wait until both values are approved, which happens at 4 − ε, and the decisions come one delay later. We checked this with an event simulation (not in Lean): scripts/latency.py. The comparisons with [9, 11, 28] in Tables 1–2 are fair only if those papers count the same way.

Typo. Algorithm 6, line 11 reads "if 1 then"; evidently "if (1) then" (as in Algorithm 4, line 11). Our liberal guards make the branch choice irrelevant.

Apart from the pseudocode slip of item 1, no statement checked here is false.

Proof-level differences (repairs)

  1. 🔧 Lemma 4.5 (validity), second part. "In order to decide 1 − v, a non-faulty party must have 1 − v in its approvedVals set (line 15)". Line 15 only requires n − t messages ⟨echo3, 1 − v⟩, so the cited justification is wrong. Repair: some uncorrupted sender of ⟨echo3, 1 − v⟩ received ⟨echo2, 1 − v⟩ from n − t parties. One of those is uncorrupted, so it approved 1 − v, which by the first part needs an uncorrupted party with input 1 − v (Alg4.validity).

  2. 🔧 Lemma 4.9 (binding) against an adaptive adversary. The proof picks "the t + 1 messages from non-faulty parties". Who is non-faulty depends on the extension, while b must be fixed at time τ. Our proof (Alg4.binding) fixes b from the prefix. If some party has broadcast ⟨echo3, u⟩ with u ≠ ⊥, take b = u and use Lemma 4.8 in the extension. Otherwise the decider's n − t echo3 messages include at least n − t − |bad(τ)| genuine ⊥'s. A later quorum ⟨echo3, w⟩ would need n − t senders among the remaining n − |G| parties plus the at most t − |bad(τ)| parties corrupted later, which is at most 2t parties. The paper's argument can be completed this way. The same repair is used for Algorithm 6 (Alg6.graded_binding, via the first ⟨echo4, ⊥⟩).

  3. Theorems 4.1, 5.1, 5.3 have no proofs in this version (deferred to the full version). We supply our own. For Algorithm 6 the binding argument has to go through the echo4 level: a grade-≥ 1 decision on v needs a genuine ⟨echo4, v⟩ (line 25 requires t + 1 of them), and the echo3 level is consistent as in Lemma 4.8.

  4. Theorem 3.1's termination argument ("at least a 50% chance") is formalized only as a counting statement. Let the adversary be deterministic, with binding values a function β of the earlier coins. Then at most k + 1 of the 2^k coin prefixes of length k leave all rounds < k without a round in which everybody commits (Alg1.rounds_count), and ∑ (k + 1)/2^k = 4 (Alg1.tsum_succ_div_two_pow). This matches the claimed 4 expected rounds. The probability space (E[T] = ∑ P(T > k), independence of the strong coin from the past) is not formalized.

Modelling choices

  1. Model (Podc/Computability/Distributed/Byzantine/AsyncBroadcast.lean). Interleaving semantics:

    • every broadcast is one event, and each delivery to each receiver is a separate event;

    • the adversary corrupts parties adaptively, at most t in total;

    • Byzantine parties can make anybody receive anything from them;

    • crashed parties stop, and their earlier broadcasts may or may not be delivered (crash during a broadcast).

    Executions are infinite (with stuttering). Fairness is weak fairness for never-corrupted parties only: their messages to each other are delivered, and a guard that stays enabled forever is eventually taken. Messages of parties corrupted later and injected messages are unconstrained. This only weakens the adversary's obligations, so the termination results are at least as strong as the paper's.

  2. Liberal guards. "Upon receiving messages from n − t parties, if they all contain v…" is modelled as "v received from n − t parties". "Not all the same" is modelled as "messages from n − t parties, and a ⊥ or two different values among them". If both branches of a "wait until" are enabled, either may be taken (the paper gives priority to branch (1)). This is a superset of the paper's deterministic behaviours, so the safety results (agreement, validity, binding, message counts) transfer. The liveness results hold for every weakly fair execution of the liberal protocol, which includes the paper's executions.

  3. Sequencing. Algorithm 4 decides only after its own echo3. Algorithm 6 sends echo(k+1) only after its own echo k (k = 3, 4) and decides only after its own echo5. This is the sequential "wait until" structure of the pseudocode. echo2 is an "upon" clause and is not required before echo3, as in the paper.

  4. No termination after deciding. A party keeps executing all clauses after deciding, so "decide" and "terminate" coincide. The paper never says when a party may stop running Algorithm 4. Lemma 4.6 explicitly assumes that no non-faulty party terminates, and the framework keeps parties running after they commit.

  5. Stronger statements than the paper's. Agreement, validity and binding are proved for all recorded decisions, including those of parties corrupted after deciding. Binding holds for every reachable state that contains a decision, which is equivalent to the paper's "first decision" form. In the Byzantine theorems, validity is stated for any set H of parties with |Hᶜ| ≤ t and no corrupted member; with adaptive corruption, H is the set of parties that are never corrupted.

  6. Crash versions (BCA-Crash, GBCA-Crash; the paper defers their full definitions to the full version). Agreement and binding are proved for all parties. Weak validity is "if all parties have input v, every decision is v (grade 2)". The bound t < ⌈n/2⌉ of Theorem 4.1 is the same as 2t < n.

  7. Framework (Algorithms 1, 2). We reason at the level of BCA instances: per round, the parties that start and finish it, their inputs, outputs and coins, with BCA agreement and validity as hypotheses. One statement covers the Byzantine case (Start = Dec = H) and the crash case. For the crash case we need weak validity for the participants of the instance ("all parties that start round r + 1 have input v"), since crashed parties never start later rounds. This is how Theorem 3.3 must read "all parties have the same input".

  8. One instance. Instance identifiers id are dropped: each protocol is a single instance.

Not formalized

  • Section 6 (BCA with threshold signatures, Theorems 6.1, 6.2), the EVBCA variants, and Theorems 4.2, 4.10, 4.11, 5.2, 5.4. These are broadcast counts of composed protocols whose constructions are in the full version.

  • Probability spaces: the ε-good and t-unpredictable coins, "termination with probability 1", the 1 + 1/ε bound of Theorems 3.4/3.5. Only the deterministic steps (Alg2.binding_step) and the counting bound for Algorithm 1 are formalized.

  • The attack on Cachin–Zanolini and the discussion of earlier protocols (full version).
