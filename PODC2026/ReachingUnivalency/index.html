Reaching Univalency with Subquadratic Communication — PODC 2026

Andrew Lewis-Pye. Reaching Univalency with Subquadratic Communication. PODC 2026. DOI 10.1145/3796701.3815918. Formalized version: arXiv 2602.05356 v1 (the same text as the proceedings version); numbering follows it. Copy of the paper: paper.pdf.

DOI · arXiv · PDF · All differences · Lean source · GitHub

Verdict: 🔧 Easy fix. Lemma 6 and the claim that the §5 protocol (Fig. 3) solves ε-BA are false, but one raised threshold and a stronger sampler condition save Theorem 1 with the same bounds. The only loss is Proposition 4 for arbitrary Extractable BA protocols, whose needed fact holds for the paper's own instance; Theorem 2 was checked only through its parts. The appendix lower bounds and Propositions 3 and 5 are proved.

Mistakes in the paper

⚠️ Proposition 4 (Extractable BA ⇒ BA)

The text after the definition claims that Consistency together with F(M_c) ∈ {0,1} implies F(M) = F(M_c) for all M_c ⊆ M ⊆ M_a, which does not follow since F(M) may be the empty string (consistency_not_monotone), so Proposition 4 is unestablished.

Impact. Theorem 2 is unaffected: the extraction function of Proposition 5 is monotone, and F(M) = v on every M that contains the correct signed outputs, within any unforgeable M_a (prop5_extract). The claim for "any finitely terminating protocol" is not saved.

Details

Also, during dissemination faulty processors may forward messages that are not in M_a. We did not decide whether Proposition 4 as printed fails for some protocol.

Repair

Requiring F to be monotone (M ⊆ M' ⊆ M_a ⇒ F(M) ⪯ F(M')) fixes this. The repaired general proposition is not in Lean.

🔧 Lemma 6 and the §5 protocol (Fig. 3)

Lemma 6 is false and the §5 protocol does not solve ε-BA: with n = 38, ε = 1/5, f = 5, k = 37 and every sample accurate to within 1/19 < ε/2 on every set, 14 correct processors output 1 and 19 output 0, while nε = 7.6 (lemma6_false, paper_protocol_fails).

Impact. Theorem 1 itself is true: with the step-1 threshold k(2/3 − ε/4) and samplers accurate also on sets that contain Byzantine processors, the paper's argument goes through (theorem1_corrected).

Details

  • What breaks. The proof needs "time-slot-1 agreement": two correct, accurately sampling processors never get different response values in step 1. With the threshold k(2/3 − ε/2) this needs perfectly representative samples. Write f/n = 1/3 − ε − η. Any sample deviation larger than about η lets the Byzantine processors push one sampler to 0 and another to 1. Here η can be arbitrarily small, while samples may deviate by up to ε/2.

  • Counterexample. n = 38, ε = 1/5, f = 5 < 38(1/3 − ε), k = 37. The 5 Byzantine processors lie in C₁ = {0..18}; C₂ = {19..37} is fault-free. In steps 1 and 2 every processor samples the 37 processors other than 13. In step 3 it samples the round's committee C_r cyclically. For every step, every processor and every set of processors, the sample deviates by at most 1/19 < ε/2. So no processor is ever bad, under §4's definition or §5's stronger "every configuration" definition.

  • The run. Before round 2, 16 correct processors have value 0 and 16 have value 1 among those sampled, so in step 4 the Byzantine processors can steer each correct processor to response 0 or 1. They make 20 respond 1 and 12 respond 0. In step 5 the 14 correct members of C₁ see 20 + 5 = 25 ≥ 2k/3 copies of 1 and keep 1 with grade 2. All members of C₂ see both values at least k/3 times, so they get grade 0 and keep their values: 16 zeros and 3 ones. C₂ then outputs 0, which ε-Validity forces since only 3 < 19ε inputs differ, and everyone but C₁ adopts 0. Final outputs: 14 correct processors output 1 and 19 output 0, while nε = 7.6.

  • Recursive version. The committees may behave as an ideal BA functionality (Counterexample.lean). They may also run the paper's own recursive protocol with a sampler at size 19 that is accurate on every set (PaperProtocolFails.lean). Either way ε-Agreement fails, whatever the sampling choices at the other sizes.

  • Larger k. Lean proves this for k = 37 only. For every k = 37m the same execution works: repeat each step-1/2 sample m times and sample the committee cyclically (j mod |C_r|). This is argued by hand, not checked in Lean.

Repair

With the step-1 threshold k(2/3 − ε/4), and samples ε/8-accurate also on sets that contain Byzantine processors, the paper's argument goes through. We prove the corrected one-level lemma, the existence of such samplers with k = k(ε), the recursion and the message bound. (lemma6_corrected, theorem1_corrected)

🔧 Lemma 6 and §5 (Byzantine processors in a sample)

The proof never bounds the Byzantine processors in a sample: "bad" only compares the fractions of correct processors with value b, so the step "a non-bad p sees ≥ 2k/3 copies of b, hence α_{2,b} > 2/3 − ε/2" ignores that these copies may come from Byzantine processors.

Impact. §5's existence claim itself is true, and our exists_sampler proves the needed form: for every set S, at most γn processors deviate by ≥ δ on S.

Details

§5's counting argument gives samplers that are good on every configuration, but Lemma 6 is stated and proved only for the actual configuration, so the property that also bounds the Byzantine processors in a sample is never used. The needed form takes only Chebyshev plus the product structure, not KL-Chernoff.

Repair

Require accuracy also on sets containing the Byzantine processors, which §5's counting over all configurations already provides (lemma6_corrected, exists_sampler).

✏️ Slips

  • Lemma 6, proof of ε-Agreement: the case split "at least nε/4 correct processors have grade 2" should be "some non-bad processor has grade 2", since in the printed negative case a non-bad processor may still keep a grade-2 value (lemma6_corrected).

Typos

  • Appendix B argues about processors: "fewer than f/2 correct processors send or receive". The step to Ω(f) messages is implicit. The proof uses only the two fault-free executions with uniform inputs, in which every message has two correct endpoints, so we get ≥ f/4 messages (appendixB_messages).

  • With samples drawn "with replacement", the deterministic protocol must count a processor sampled twice twice. Fig. 3 ("send your value to every processor that samples you") leaves this implicit.

The formalizationmodel, Lean names of the results, trust

Model.

  • Lower bounds. CSLib's synchronous lock-step executions OmExec (Cslib.Byzantine.PartialSync):

    • an execution is the inputs plus the set of delivered batches;

    • processes are arbitrary deterministic state machines;

    • message complexity counts messages from correct processes to others.

  • Theorem 7. Omission faults (Admits). Faulty processes run the protocol, so signatures are irrelevant and the authenticated case is covered.

  • Appendix B. Crash faults (CrashAdm): a crashing process delivers an arbitrary subset of its batches at its crash time and nothing afterwards. P₁; P₂ is one algorithm whose first phase is the time interval < t*. Univalency is the paper's: every admissible execution extending the prefix before t* decides the same value.

  • ε-RPK.

    • Lock-step, Byzantine faults, static adversary, deterministic. The two committees are C₁, C₂.

    • A sampling choice lists, for each round, step and processor, k sampled processors (repetitions counted).

    • Byzantine processors answer each sampler arbitrarily (Option Bool, where none is ⊥ or silence). Step-3 ties are broken adversarially.

    • One level (RPK.lean) treats committee instances as black boxes that must satisfy ε-BA only within their fault bound, as §4 does.

    • The recursion (Recursive.lean) runs the protocol itself on the committees, re-indexed. The Byzantine behaviour inside a committee may depend on all values. On n ≤ 2 processors a trivial base protocol runs; there are no faults there.

  • Complexity. Each sample is one message carrying a value in {0,1,⊥}. The count 6kn per level plus the recursive calls is a recurrence that follows the protocol's structure; it is not derived from an operational message semantics.

Remarks.

  • Theorem 7 assumes f < n − 1. The implication holds for f ≤ n; for f ≥ n − 1 its hypothesis cannot be satisfied.

  • Footnote 6 lets t* depend on the inputs; we fix t*.
