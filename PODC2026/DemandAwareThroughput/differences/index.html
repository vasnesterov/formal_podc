Differences between the formalization and the paper

Rendered from differencies.md of Demand-Aware vs. Demand-Oblivious Throughput — PODC 2026.

Paper: Bentert, Avin, Schmid, A Separation Between Optimal Demand-Oblivious and Demand-Aware Network Throughput, PODC 2026, arXiv 2605.04699v1.

Modelling choices

  1. Multigraphs and capacities. A network is an ℕ-adjacency matrix. A (2n−1)-regular network has all row and column sums equal to 2n−1. As in the paper's "more formally" paragraph (§1.1), arcs get capacity 1 and the demand is scaled by 2n−1.

  2. Paths and the capacity charge. A path is a vertex sequence src :: rest with rest ≠ [], i.e. at least one arc, as in the paper. The paper's capacity constraint is per arc of the multigraph: ∑_i 1_{e∈P_i} d_i ≤ 1, where the arc identities of P_i are part of the flow. We do not model arc identities. Instead we use two aggregated semantics:

    • CanRoute: on the pair (u, v), ∑_i d_i · #{times P_i traverses (u,v)} ≤ A u v. This is at least as strict as the paper's rule: the amounts can be split fractionally over the A u v parallel arcs, filling them one after another, and reusing an arc is only cheaper in the paper. All yes-instances of the multi-hop problems are proved for it: Theorem 5, Observation 3 and example_M1_throughput.

    • CanRouteRel: ∑_i d_i · 1[(u,v) ∈ P_i] ≤ A u v. The paper's rule implies it, since a path through (u,v) uses at least one of the A u v parallel arcs. All no-instances of the multi-hop problems are proved for it (proposition_1_rel, proposition_4_weak_rel, example_M2_not_throughput_rel), and DAThroughput.rel transfers them to CanRoute.

    • For direct routing (single arcs) the three semantics coincide (canRouteDirectly_iff: 0 ≤ D ≤ A entrywise).

    Hence every checked statement transfers to the paper's exact semantics.

  3. Throughput as a maximum. The paper defines throughput as "the largest θ such that G can host θM". We render "throughput ≥ κ" as "∃ θ ≥ κ such that G hosts θM". This is the same as "max ≥ κ" whenever the maximum exists, as the paper assumes. Hosting is downward closed for κ ≥ 0 (CanHost.of_le). The weak variants likewise read "∃ M' ≤ M hosted with ∑M'/n ≥ κ".

  4. Range of κ. The problems take κ ∈ [0, 1]; our definitions accept any real κ.

    • Proposition 1 is stated for all κ > 5/6. The paper's matrix M_κ needs ε = κ − 5/6 ≤ 1/6; for κ > 1 we use the matrix of κ = 1.

    • Observation 2 needs 0 ≤ κ ≤ 1 (as in the paper) and n ≥ 1.

    • Propositions 2 and 3, Observation 3 and Theorem 6 need n ≥ 1; n = 0 is degenerate.

  5. Randomness. Theorems 3 and 4 are stated on finite probability spaces (FinDist: a non-negative weight function summing to 1). "With probability 1" means "for every outcome of positive weight". Theorem 4 is stated for an arbitrary finite index type instead of [t]. Theorem 3 is the special case the paper uses: n × n matrices with integer row and column sums.

  6. Theorem 2 / Theorem 5 conclusion. theorem_2 concludes DAThroughput M (5/8 − ε). For ε > 5/8 this is vacuous (negative target), consistent with the problem definition.

Proofs that differ from the paper

  1. Proposition 2. The paper uses the greedy algorithm. We pad ⌈n·M_ij⌉, whose row and column sums are < 2n and hence ≤ 2n−1, to a regular matrix with CSLib's Matrix.exists_regular_above. The paper's greedy argument is not checked. It also needs a small care point: one should consider whichever of row i* and column j* becomes full first. Randomized exhaustive comparisons for n ≤ 4 (script in the work folder) found the greedy optimal; this is not a proof.

  2. Observation 3 is derived from Proposition 2 and Observation 1, not from the external Theorem 1 of Keslassy et al. (which is not formalized). Theorem 5's case κ ≤ n/(2n−1) uses the same route.

  3. Proposition 1 and Proposition 4 (first part) use two cut inequalities valid for every flow on two nodes (two_node_bounds_rel), instead of the paper's per-graph reasoning: D_ab ≤ A_ab and D_ab + D_aa + D_bb ≤ A_ab + A_aa + A_bb. For the second part of Proposition 4 we use the per-entry bound min(e, A) ≤ A/2 + e/2 − 1/4 (+1/4 on the diagonal for odd n), instead of counting "fully usable arcs".

  4. Theorem 3 and Lemma 1. The paper (and Gandhi et al.) find a simple cycle in the bipartite graph of fractional entries. We instead obtain a direction with zero line sums by a dimension count (exists_circulation). For Theorem 3, a minimal-support direction has at most two nonzero entries per line (exists_cycle_direction), which is all the negative-correlation step needs. In Lemma 1 the sign of the direction is chosen so that ∑ a·c does not decrease. We use ∑ x² ≥ X²/n² (Cauchy–Schwarz) as in the paper.

Errors and gaps in the paper

  1. Theorem 5, application of Theorem 4 (🔧). "t = n + Y_i" with Y_i = ∑_j (1 − b'_ij) treats Y_i as an integer number of constant variables of weight 1, but Y_i is not an integer in general. Repair: n constant variables (X ≡ 1, x = 1) of weight 1 − b'_ij (line_bad_prob_le). This is a claim inside a proof, not a statement.

  2. Theorem 5, choice of n_κ (routine gap). The conditions are imposed at n_κ only and used for all n ≥ n_κ. They do hold for all n ≥ n_κ: 2n e^{−cn} is > 1 up to its maximum at 1/c and decreasing afterwards, for c = 2ε²/3 small. Formalized via eventually_large.

  3. Theorem 5, sign typo (✏️). "ε = κ − 5/8 > 0" should be ε = 5/8 − κ. Also "b_ij < (2n−1)κ a_ij" is only ≤ when a_ij = 0, and "b_ij < min(a_ij, d_ij)" should be b_ij < d_ij. These are inside a proof.

  4. Proposition 1's proof (🔧).

    • The equation 5/6 − 5ε/12 − ε²/2 = 5/6 − 5ε/6 + ε − ε − ε²/2 is false. The final inequality is still true: 5/6 − 5ε/12 − ε²/2 < (5/6+ε)(1−ε) iff ε²/2 < 7ε/12.

    • The bottom graphs are dismissed without a real argument ("since 1 − ε > 2/3").

    The statement is true.

  5. Problem definition typo (✏️). The box for Demand-Aware Weak Direct Throughput asks whether "the direct throughput of G … is at least κ". Read literally, the problem is Demand-Aware Direct Throughput, and Theorem 6 (κ ≤ (7n−4)/(8n−4) ⇒ yes) would contradict Proposition 3 for n ≥ 2. The intended "weak direct throughput" is defined in §1.1 and is clear from the problem's name, Observation 4 and the proof of Theorem 6; the fix is one word. We formalize the intended problem (DAWeakDirectThroughput).

  6. Proposition 3 typo (✏️). The statement constructs M_κ but says "(M, κ) is a no-instance" (checked on the rendered page; M carries no subscript). The intent (M_κ, κ) is evident from the proof. Both items are pure presentation typos, but item 15 makes Theorem 6 false if read at the paper's word.

  7. §2 example (no error). pdftotext renders the stacked fraction of "(M₁, 8/9) is a yes-instance of Demand-Aware Throughput" as "98"; the rendered page reads 8/9, which we prove (example_M1_throughput, example_M1_not_weakDirect).

  8. Numerical remark after Theorem 5. "For n ≥ 26000 … κ = 0.6": the proof's two conditions first hold at n = 26068. The other values check out: improvement over n/(2n−1) from n ≈ 701, κ = 0.55 from n = 2243 ≤ 2250, κ = 0.624 at ≈ 26.7 million. The two conditions are only sufficient, so the claim for 26000 ≤ n < 26068 is unsupported rather than refuted; it is a remark, not a statement.
