Differences between the formalization and the paper

Rendered from differencies.md of Consistent Refereeing: Impossibilities for Strong Linearizability — PODC 2026.

Paper: Attiya, Castañeda, Enea. Impossibility Results for Strong Linearizability: The Difficulty of Consistent Refereeing. PODC 2026 (arXiv 2506.18401 v3, identical text). Classification in brackets.

Model (all sections)

  1. Model. CSLib's asynchronous shared-memory model (Cslib.SharedMemory.Impl): deterministic processes, atomic base objects with deterministic sequential specifications, executions = label lists (invocation / internal step / response). Responses are separate steps that do not access memory (harmless generalization). No crashes are modelled explicitly; a crashed process is one that stops taking steps, which the progress conditions on infinite executions cover. [modelling choice]

  2. Caller identity. Objects cannot see the caller, so compete carries the caller's id (ContestOp.compete i, invoked by i); roles are enforced by the admissible executions and the implementation must allow the role operations (hallow). [modelling choice]

  3. Restricted hypotheses. For the one-shot contest object, strong linearizability and lock-freedom are only assumed on admissible executions (Adm: each process invokes at most once, the operation of its role, the referee only after some compete completed — exactly the executions the proof of Theorem 5 considers). For the long-lived contest object, on executions where competitors only invoke compete and the referee invokes decide at most once (LLAdm). These are weaker hypotheses than the paper's, so the impossibilities are stronger; versions for the unrestricted notions are derived. [strengthening]

  4. Classes of base objects. Instead of concrete objects we use semantic classes: Herlihy's interfering objects (per state: any two operations commute or one overwrites the other), window-like objects with window w (every operation is read-only or a blind write, any w consecutive non-read-only operations determine the state; w-window registers are window-like with any w' ≥ w), stack-like objects (isomorphic to a stack). windowObj, stackObj and registers are proved to belong to them. [generalization]

Section 3–4 (one-shot contest, queues)

  1. Theorem 5: wait-free vs lock-free. The statement of Theorem 5 says "no wait-free"; the abstract intro ("no lock-free", Section 1), Corollary 12 and the proof only use lock-freedom (and the proof's "due to wait-freedom" in Claims 6 and 10 needs only lock-freedom, since operations are one-shot). We prove the lock-free version and derive the wait-free one. On one-shot (admissible) executions the two notions coincide (finitely many operations), so the paper's two phrasings agree; theorem5_lockFree is stronger only for the unrestricted notions of our multi-shot model. [not an error; modelling remark]

  2. Theorem 5, stack case (4b/4c): "w.l.o.g. pushed values are distinct". The paper modifies the algorithm by stamping pushed values (footnote 3: "does not affect A's correctness and progress properties"). Our proof needs no such assumption: it runs the canonical extension of α·i·j and of α·j in lock-step, tracking stack positions; at the first pop into the critical part the configurations α·i·j·β·q and α·j·β·q·i are literally equal. The paper's case split (i)–(iii) on what p_k and p_0 pop is replaced by this single argument. Footnote 3 (stamping) is a standard, sound w.l.o.g.; we avoid it only because stamping is awkward for our semantic class of stack-like objects. [formalization route; the paper's step is fine]

  3. Theorem 5, case 4c description ("conceive the next step of p_i as a push of u_i", u_i the top of R in p_i(C)) is hand-wavy: the two orders of the pop and the push leave u_j·T and s₀·T (s₀·T the stack in C), not the shapes of case 4b. Covered by the argument of item 6. [imprecise proof, statement true]

  4. Claim 10 also has to handle steps that are invocations or responses (the paper's processes take "steps" that may be invocations of compete); they do not change the memory and are handled like reads (case 3a). The case "both steps write the same window register" requires showing that every other competitor writes to it too; the paper does this via cases 1 and 3a, we via the full pair analysis (also covering interfering objects and stacks). [detail]

  5. Proposition 11. The paper's "due to strong linearizability we can assume that A is an atomic base object" is a composition theorem not available in our framework; we prove the reduction concretely by translating executions label by label. The resulting contest implementation is wait-free only on one-shot executions, and its strong linearizability needs the referee to decide at most once; the reduction needs an injective encoding of process ids as queue values and the queue must allow those operations. The contest object is one-shot by definition (Section 4), so this is the paper's setting. [not an error; restriction needed only because our model is multi-shot]

  6. Theorem 4. If competitors may invoke compete repeatedly, the paper's algorithm (write on every compete) overflows the (n−1)-window. Our implementation writes only on the first compete of each competitor (a persistent local bit), and is then strongly linearizable on all executions. The hypothesis n ≥ 2 is not needed. On one-shot executions our algorithm coincides with the paper's; the contest object is one-shot by definition, so the paper's claim is correct as stated. [not an error; strengthening]

  7. Theorem 3. We prove wait-freedom and linearizability for executions in which the referee decides at most once; with two decides the algorithm is not linearizable. Decisive linearizability is not defined in our framework and is not formalized. (Two decides are outside the paper's one-shot object.) [not formalized: decisive linearizability]

  8. Theorem 1 is obtained as a special case of Corollary 12 (base objects all interfering); its own proof (three enqueuers, one dequeuer) is not formalized separately. Through this route Theorem 1 (like Corollary 12) assumes an injective encoding P → V of process ids as queue values, i.e. at least n values; the paper's own proof of Theorem 1 needs only 3. [route; slightly weaker]

Section 5 (long-lived contest)

  1. Model. Competitors-only extensions are schedules (List P / ℕ → P): in an admissible execution every process has at most one possible next label, so nothing is lost. Indistinguishability is agreement of the final configurations (memory, local state and pending operation of the observers, CSLib's AgreeOn). Invocations and responses are steps that do not access memory; the paper's case analyses (Lemmas 20, 23, 27, 28, Theorems 25, 30) omit them, ours treat them (like reads or as commuting steps). [under-specified case analyses]

  2. Long-lived contest specification is nondeterministic (decide may return any x bounding all competitors' counts): llSpec, strong linearizability w.r.t. a nondeterministic specification (linearizations carry responses). [faithful]

  3. Lemma 17 needs decide to be pending in α (with configuration-based indistinguishability); in its proof the operations of q must be counted inside β' (α' may contain a different number of operations of q). [under-specified / proof detail]

  4. Lemmas 19, 22, 24, 28, 29: "due to wait-freedom" means: in an infinite competitors-only extension some competitor is scheduled infinitely often and so completes infinitely many operations (resps_unbounded). [proof detail]

  5. Theorem 25, last paragraph ("We have that αβp is closed. Otherwise, if … contradicting Lemma 24(1)") is garbled; the intended argument (Lemma 23 contradicts Lemma 24(1) unless every competitor writes R and the referee reads R) is formalized. [typo-level]

  6. Theorem 30, case "all processes T&S the same object R": proof gap, statement true. The paper claims that αβγpγ′₁q′ᵏγ′₂ and αβγ′₁q′ᵏγpγ′₂ have the same shared memory and are indistinguishable to any q″ ≠ q, q′. If R is still unset after αβ, q wins the test&set in the first execution and q′ in the second; the k further steps of q′ (and those of γ′₂) may then write different values elsewhere, which q″ can read. Fixed by a new argument (const_valency_contra): once a competitor has set R, every later step of the referee on R leaves the memory unchanged and commutes with every competitor step, so by Lemma 17 the singleton valency W(αβq·u·p) does not depend on the competitors-only extension u; choosing u in which a competitor completes more than that value contradicts the specification. Lemma 29(2) is not needed for this case. [proof gap, statement true]

  7. Lemma 28, test&set case: "αβq and αβq′ are closed" — αβq′ is supervalent there; only αβq needs to be closed. Lemma 29: the last line cites Lemma 21 instead of 27. [typos]

  8. Lemma 29(2) for w = 0: the required sequences (|γ| = w, γ = qλ) do not exist; we use window max w 1 (a 0-window object is also 1-window-like). [false only in a degenerate case]

  9. Generality. Theorem 25 holds for any base objects whose primitives are read-only or blind writes; Theorem 30 for any window-like (window w) and test&set-like objects (every primitive sets the object to a fixed state; readable test&set is not covered, as in the paper). [generalization]

  10. Lemma 14 is formalized for an atomic counter, max register and snapshot (the long-lived contest is implemented from one atomic object); the fetch&increment-only / fetch&add variants and the "from a strongly linearizable implementation" part are not. Corollaries 26 and 31 are not formalized: they need a general composition theorem for strongly linearizable implementations, which the framework lacks (for queues we did the composition concretely, Proposition 11). [not formalized]
