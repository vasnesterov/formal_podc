Differences between the formalization and the paper

Rendered from differencies.md of Average Complexity for the LOCAL Model — PODC 2015.

Paper: L. Feuilloley, Brief Announcement: Average Complexity for the LOCAL Model, PODC 2015 (arXiv 1505.05072v1, identical text).

Model

  1. Oriented rings. We formalize rings with a consistent orientation (IdRing; node v + 1 is the right neighbour of v), and views are the sequences j ↦ id (v + j). The paper's LOCAL model on cycles has no orientation. Every algorithm for unoriented rings is an algorithm for oriented rings with the same radii, so the lower bound (Theorem 1) for oriented rings implies the one for unoriented rings. Lemmas 2 and 3 are proved for minimality within the class of oriented algorithms (which is what the oriented Theorem 1 needs).

  2. Views wrap around. For 2r + 1 > n the view of radius r is the periodic sequence, not the ball as a graph. Both carry the same information (the ball of radius ⌊n/2⌋ is the whole ring, which determines the periodic sequence, and conversely).

  3. Algorithms (Alg C) decide at each radius r from the view restricted to [-r, r] whether to stop and with which output; the radius r(v) is the first radius at which the node stops (Alg.rad). They do not know n (as in the paper). Identifiers are arbitrary distinct naturals, and validity (Alg.Valid) is required on all rings of all sizes n ≥ 3 with all identifier assignments. The paper's construction implicitly needs this too (it runs the same algorithm on smaller cycles whose identifiers are a subset of {1, …, n}).

  4. Theorem 1's ring uses identifiers {0, …, n - 1} (a permutation of them), the paper's "permutation of the identifiers". The total radius is summed over positions 0, …, n - 1.

  5. Lemma 2 is stated in the paper for "a graph G with identifiers"; only cycles are meant and only cycles are formalized.

Gaps and errors in the paper's proof (all repaired; Theorem 1 holds)

  1. "Without loss of generality … only for minimal algorithms" is unjustified. The paper gives no argument that a minimal algorithm below a given one exists. There are infinitely many neighbourhoods, so a descending sequence of improvements need not stop, and the limit of a chain of algorithms is not obviously an algorithm (the outputs need not converge). We prove existence by Zorn's lemma on radius functions plus a compactness argument (Tychonoff) for the outputs of the limit algorithm (Alg.exists_isMinimal_le, exists_valid_le_of_isChain, Minimal.lean).

  2. Lemma 3's reduction from 3- to 4-colouring is invalid. Lemma 2 is proved for algorithms that are minimal among 4-colouring algorithms. Lemma 3 is stated for algorithms minimal among 3-colouring algorithms, and its proof says "it is sufficient to prove the result for 4-colouring, as it implies the result for 3-colouring". But a 3-colouring algorithm that no 3-colouring algorithm improves may still be improved by a 4-colouring algorithm (Lemma 2's modified algorithm uses all 4 colours), so Lemma 2 does not apply to it. We do not know whether Lemma 3 as stated (minimal for 3-colouring) is true; we neither prove nor refute it. Repair: we state Lemma 3 for minimal 4-colouring algorithms and prove Theorem 1 for all 4-colouring algorithms (theorem_1_four). A 3-colouring algorithm is a 4-colouring algorithm with the same radii (Alg.map, Valid.map), which gives the 3-colouring statement (theorem_1_three, theorem_1). This needs Linial's bound for 4-colourings rather than 3-colourings (Alg.exists_lt_radAt_four).

  3. Lemma 2: ring size. We assume the ring has at least 2T + 1 nodes, where T = max {r(x), r(y)} + k is the threshold, so the radius-T views do not wrap. The modified algorithm A' then also checks that the 2T + 1 identifiers it sees are distinct. This makes the node's offset d from x well defined. The paper's proof ignores wrap-around. Lemma 3 and Theorem 1 only use the lemma in this case. When 2T + 1 > n and 2r + 1 ≤ n, the inequality r ≤ T that Lemma 3 needs holds trivially. We have not checked whether Lemma 2 holds without this hypothesis.

  4. Lemma 2: orientation. The paper picks the orientation "w.l.o.g. ID(x) > ID(y)". With oriented rings, x is simply the left end. The paper's d is "the distance between v and x"; we use the offset along the segment from x to y. The colour rules are the paper's. A neighbour that stopped strictly before the threshold is avoided. If some neighbour did not stop before the threshold, the colour is taken in {0, 1} (even d) or {2, 3} (odd d). When both neighbours stopped early, any colour avoiding both is chosen (the paper leaves this case implicit).

  5. Lemma 3: r versus r(v). We assume r ≤ r(v) instead of r = r(v) and require 2r + 1 ≤ n. The paper applies Lemma 3 in Theorem 1 with r = ½ log*(n/2), and the centre's radius is only known to be at least that. With r = r(v) the ball of radius r(v)/2 could exceed the slice, so this is the form the proof needs. The quantitative form is r + h (r - h) ≤ ∑_{|j| ≤ h} r(v + j) with h = ⌊r/2⌋, and the average over the 2h + 1 nodes is at least r / 4. The paper only writes Ω(r).

  6. Linial's bound as a black box. The paper cites "for every algorithm that 3-colours a cycle of length larger than n/2, there exists a permutation of the identifiers such that at least one vertex needs a radius of ½ log*(n/2)". The exact constant (no - O(1)) is not justified by the citation. We prove our own version from scratch, for any number of colours, with the shift-graph argument: if |S| > tower (2t + 2), every valid 4-colouring algorithm has a node of radius > t on some ring with identifier set S (Alg.exists_lt_radAt_four; general C: (2^·)^[2t] |C| < |S|). We use R = ⌊(log*(n/2) - 1)/2⌋ in place of ½ log*(n/2).

  7. Constants in Theorem 1. The paper states Ω(log* n). We prove the explicit bounds n ⌊(log*(n/2) - 1)/2⌋ ≤ 16 ∑_v r(v) and n (log* n - 3) ≤ 32 ∑_v r(v) for every n ≥ 3 (theorem_1_three, theorem_1), and the average form (log* n - 3)/32 ≤ (∑_v r(v))/n (theorem_1_average).

  8. Slice bookkeeping in Theorem 1. The paper removes "a ½ log*(n/2)-ball around a vertex with large radius" until fewer than n/2 identifiers remain. We remove slices of exactly 2R + 1 identifiers while more than ⌊n/2⌋ remain (exists_slices). The remaining identifiers are appended in increasing order. We note that the slice must be the ball in the ring produced by Linial's bound for the remaining identifiers, and that the centre's radius in π is ≥ R because its views of radius < R are unchanged (the paper says the whole R-neighbourhood is "exactly the same", which is what we use).

Section 2 (largest ID)

  1. "Has seen all the cycle" (the stopping rule of the straightforward algorithm) is made precise as "some identifier appears twice in the window". A node that does not know n has no other way to tell it has seen the whole ring. Correctness of the algorithm is proved (LargestId.straightforward_correct).

  2. Worst case. The paper says the maximum "needs n/2 rounds". We prove that for every correct algorithm, on every ring the maximum node has radius ≥ ⌊n/2⌋ (LargestId.half_le_radAt_of_max).

  3. Average O(log n): different proof. The paper uses the recurrence a(p) = max_{1≤k≤⌈p/2⌉} {k + a(k−1) + a(p−k)} for the worst-case total radius on a segment, and cites OEIS A000788 for a(p) = Θ(p ln p). Neither the recurrence's correctness nor the asymptotics are proved there. We do not formalize them. We only define a (LargestId.segA) and check a(0..15) against A000788 (segA_eq_A000788). Instead, we prove directly that two nodes of radius ≥ d are at cyclic distance ≥ d, hence ∑_v r(v) ≤ ∑_{d=1}^n n/d ≤ n (⌊log₂ n⌋ + 1) (LargestId.sum_radAt_straightforward_le). This gives the paper's conclusion: the average radius is O(log n).

  4. The paper's "the radius needed by these other vertices is the minimum distance to a vertex with larger ID or an endpoint" is used only implicitly: our radius is the actual stopping radius of the formal algorithm.
