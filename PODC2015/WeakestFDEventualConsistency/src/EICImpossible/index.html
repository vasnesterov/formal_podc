So under the literal reading, EC and EIC are *not* equivalent, and Lemma 4 (a transformation from EC to EIC) is false; Theorem 3 holds only with EIC-Agreement among correct processes (`Alg6.lemma4`, `Alg7.lemma5`). **Argument.** Fix distinct processes `a`, `b`, `c`; `a` proposes `false`, `b` proposes `true`. * In the *solo run* of `a` (all others crashed from the start, `Ω` outputs `a` at `a`), `a` must respond to instance `0` (EIC-Termination), at some first time `T_a`, with its own proposal `false` (EIC-Validity: nobody else ever invokes anything). Likewise `b` responds `true` at `T_b` in its solo run. * In the *merged run*, `a` and `b` take exactly the steps of their solo runs until `T_a`, resp. `T_b`, and then crash; `c` is correct, `Ω` outputs `c` at `c`; the messages between the three are delayed until `a` and `b` have crashed (they are faulty, so they need not receive anything). `a` and `b` cannot distinguish the merged run from their solo runs (`sim`), so they return `false` and `true` to instance `0` and these remain their last responses forever: they return infinitely different values. **Model.** The application is folded into the algorithm: the initial state of `p` depends only on `p`'s own proposals `inp p : ℕ → V` (`EICAlg.init`), so a process does not know the others' proposals. Process `p` is deemed to invoke instance `0` at its first step and instance `j + 1` at the step of its first response to `j` (the paper's usage assumption), with value `inp p j` (`hist`).
