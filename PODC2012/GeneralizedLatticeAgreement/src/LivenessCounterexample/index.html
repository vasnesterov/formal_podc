Process `2` is faulty; `0` and `1` are correct (a majority). The lattice is `Finset ℕ` (the power-set lattice used throughout the paper). Process `2`'s acceptor acknowledges proposer `0`'s proposal `{1,2}` and crashes in the middle of this `Accept` step: its `ACK` reaches proposer `0` (which decides) but not the learners. The third acceptor (process `1`) rejects `{1,2}`, because it had already accepted `{3}` (from process `2`). So the learners see only one `ACK` for `{1,2}`, and the proposers, both passive and with their buffers already included in their `proposedValue`s (through late `NACK`s), never propose again. The learners keep `learntValue = {2}`, and the value `{1}` received by the correct process `0` is never learnt. One might read a guard `status = active` into `ProcessNACK` as intended. This execution does not depend on it: the learners never see a quorum of `ACK`s because the faulty acceptor `2` acknowledges the proposals of both correct proposers, and its copies of these `ACK`s for the learners are lost (the paper's assumption (d) only covers messages *between correct processes*; its failure model includes message loss). Inputs are singletons, and every message from a correct process to a correct process is processed (`AllCorrectDelivered`). The correct learners stay at `learntValue = ⊥`. `run_trNoNack` shows that the same schedule without the two late `ProcessNACK` steps is also a run, ending with both correct proposers passive and nothing to propose; the only messages between correct processes left unprocessed are the `NACK`s to these passive proposers.
