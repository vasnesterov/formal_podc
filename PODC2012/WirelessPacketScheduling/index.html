Dynamic Packet Scheduling in Wireless Networks — PODC 2012

Thomas Kesselheim. Dynamic Packet Scheduling in Wireless Networks. PODC 2012. DOI 10.1145/2332432.2332487. Formalized version: arXiv 1203.1226 v1; numbering follows it.

DOI · arXiv · PDF · All differences · Lean source · GitHub

Verdict: ⚠️ Weakened. Theorem 1, the transformation (Algorithm 1) that makes static O(I log n)-type algorithms scale linearly in I and the first of the paper's two steps, is false: there are instances satisfying its hypothesis on which Algorithm 1 fails with probability > 0.98, while the theorem claims failure probability ≤ 1/n^φ. No repair is proved, so the results that go through it (Cor. 13, 14) are unestablished, and the second step, the stable protocol of Section 4, was not checked. Theorem 20 (local clocks cost a factor m/(2 ln m)) holds for m ≥ 4 after a repair of its proof.

Mistakes in the paper

❌ Theorem 1 (Algorithm 1 does not halve the interference)

Theorem 1 is false: packet routing on m = 200 links with 5100 packets per link, φ = 1 and an "all or nothing" static algorithm satisfies the hypothesis, yet Algorithm 1 fails with probability > 0.98 instead of ≤ 1/n^φ (theorem1_false, theorem1_false_ln).

Impact. The first step of the paper's transformation fails for its own algorithm. Section 4 is not touched when it starts from an f(m)·I + g(m,n) algorithm. But all corollaries that go through Algorithm 1 (Cor. 13, 14, Thm 19 + Sec. 7.2) are unestablished as written. They very likely hold up to constants: doubling the number of delay classes probably repairs Theorem 1, with leading constant 4 f(mχ) instead of 2 f(mχ) (not formalized).

Details

Iteration i gives every packet a uniform delay in [ψ], ψ = ⌈2^{-i+1} I/χ⌉. The proof of Claim 2 asserts that a delay class has expected load E[(W R_j)(e)] ≤ ‖W R^{(i-1)}‖/ψ ≤ χ/2. In fact the bound is only χ. So a constant fraction of the classes exceeds χ, and 𝒜(χ, mχ) gives no guarantee for them. Counterexample (in Lean):

  • Packet routing (W = I) on m = 200 links, with 5100 packets per link. So n = 1 020 000 and I = 5100.

  • φ = 1.

  • The static algorithm is "all or nothing". If ‖W·R_S‖∞ ≤ I', it sends all packets, one per link and step, in I' steps (f ≡ 1). Otherwise it sends nothing. It meets the hypothesis with success probability 1. The paper's own proof treats overloaded classes in exactly this way (Y_j).

Then 85 < χ < 86, ξ = 1 and ψ = 60. On each link, a delay class gets Bin(5100, 1/60) packets, with mean 85. With probability ≥ 1/25 this is exactly 86 > χ, independently over the 200 links. So all 60 classes fail with probability ≥ 1 − 60·(24/25)^200 > 0.98, and nothing is delivered. The clean-up runs 𝒜(2φχ log n, n) then face interference 5100 > 2φχ log n and deliver nothing either. The statement fails for log = log₂ and for log = ln.

The counterexample uses the freedom the hypothesis leaves on overloaded inputs: a static algorithm that still delivers most packets of an overloaded class would not fail here. But the theorem is claimed for every algorithm meeting the hypothesis, and the proof itself counts overloaded classes as lost. A Monte Carlo run on a single link with the all-or-nothing algorithm (simulation/sim.py, φ = 1) shows the failure is the typical behaviour, not an artefact of the chosen sizes. The failure probability is ≈ 0.57 for n = 2376, ≈ 0.78 for n = 20000 and ≈ 0.99 for n = 10⁵. Once a round leaves more than half of the load, the next round's classes are overloaded on average, and the process stalls.

❌ Claim 2, proof: ψ ≥ 4(ξ − i + 2φ log n)

The claimed inequality is false for the last iterations: for i = ξ, the definition of ξ only gives ψ > 2φ log n.

Impact. A separate wrong step in the proof of Claim 2 (not formalized). A larger constant in ξ probably repairs it, but no repair is proved.

❌ Claim 2, proof: bound on ‖W R^{(i)}‖

‖W R^{(i)}‖ ≤ χ ∑(Y_j + Z_j) < χψ/2 gives 2^{-i} I + χ/2, not 2^{-i} I, because of the ceiling in ψ.

Impact. A separate wrong step in the proof of Claim 2 (not formalized): the exact halving is not recovered by anything proved.

🔧 Theorem 20 for m ≤ 3 (local clocks)

For m = 2, 3 the proof's intermediate claim "no local-clock protocol is stable for λ ≥ ln m/m" is false on the network of Figure 1, so Theorem 20 is unestablished there, not refuted (immediate_stable_two, immediate_stable_three).

Impact. A corner of an asymptotic statement; Theorem 20 holds for every m ≥ 4 (theorem_20, see Proof gaps).

Details

Short links can send each packet in its arrival slot. Then the long link gets i.i.d. opportunities of probability (1−λ)^{m−1} > λ, so its queue is stable at λ = ln m/m. So the network of Figure 1 cannot prove Theorem 20 for m ≤ 3, and the statement is open there.

✏️ Slips

  • Theorem 1 states the middle term of its step bound as O(log n · f(mχ)), but ξ f(mχ) χ = O(log n · log m · f(mχ)), since χ = Θ(log m). The displayed estimate ≤ 2 f(mχ) I + ξ f(mχ) χ + (⌈φ⌉+1) f(n) 2φχ log n is correct (alg1Time_le), and the introduction states the correct O(I log m + log n log² m + log² n log m).

  • Proof of Theorem 1: "2^{-ψ/4} ≤ 2^{ξ−i+2φ log n}" should have a minus sign, and the factor 2^ξ/2^i in Claim 2 should be 2^i/2^ξ.

  • "for each constant φ ≥ 0" needs φ > 0.

  • Lemma 7: Pr[∆ = −1] = 1 should be q, as used in the proof.

Typos

  • Proof of Theorem 20: the expected number of transmissions in a window of length k should be ≥ (1−1/m)λk, not (1−1/m)λ.

  • "one of the last φ log n executions" should be ⌈φ⌉ + 1.

  • Proof of Claim 2: the superscript in (W · R_j^{(i+1)})(e) should be i − 1.

Proof gaps in true statements (2)Theorem 20, proof: final chain at λ = ln m/m · Theorem 20, proof: endpoint not excluded

🔧 Theorem 20, proof: final chain at λ = ln m/m

The proof's final chain (1 − (1−1/m)λ)^{m−1} < 1/e^{ln m} ≤ λ at λ = ln m/m is false.

Impact. Statement true for every m ≥ 4; repaired (key_ineq, theorem_20).

Details

The left side is about m^{-(1-1/m)²} > 1/m. For m = 4 it is even > λ (paper_step_fails_four).

Repair

Let the window length k grow, so that the factor 1 − 1/m disappears, leaving (1 − λ)^{m−1} < λ (key_ineq).

🔧 Theorem 20, proof: endpoint not excluded

The argument shows instability only for λ ≥ ln m/m, while m/(2 ln m)-competitiveness asks for stability only at λ < ln m/m, so the conclusion does not follow as written.

Impact. Statement true for every m ≥ 4; repaired (exists_rate_below, theorem_20).

Repair

Use continuity. We prove that for every m ≥ 4 there is λ < ln m/m at which no protocol with local clocks is stable (theorem_20). The protocol may be arbitrary, and the long link even omniscient.

The formalizationmodel, Lean names of the results, trust

Model.

  • Theorem 1 (static, single hop). An instance has links E, packets P (each on one link) and W ∈ [0,1]^{E×E} with W_ee = 1. A static algorithm is a black box 𝒜(I', n') : Finset P → PMF (Finset P) that returns the undelivered packets. Its hypothesis is: it delivers everything with probability ≥ 1 − 1/n' when ‖W R_S‖∞ ≤ I' and |S| ≤ n'. Algorithm 1 is written verbatim as a PMF, with independent uniform delays and independent runs per class; m = |E| (D = 1). The base of log is a parameter; the counterexample covers 2 and e. The step bound is the deterministic schedule length.

  • Theorem 20. Discrete time; network of Figure 1 (m − 1 short links, one long link); Bernoulli arrivals with expected rate λ.

    • Acknowledgement-based with only a local clock means: a short link's execution (transmissions, arrivals, deliveries, queue) is a process of its own randomness in local time. Its feedback is always "success". The links are independent (product measure). Link i is switched on at an adversarial global time d_i and is silent before. This is the paper's "delaying the start does not change the behaviour".

    • The long link is an arbitrary process on any probability space extending the short links' randomness. Its successes need all short links silent.

    • Stability = bounded expected queue length (Section 4.1).

    • The positive results (TDMA with a global clock, the immediate protocol) are stated for the long link's queue as a Markov chain. Its slot model: arrival, then possibly a transmission in the same slot.
