import Papers.PODC2012.TMLiveness.Safety /-! # The environment's strategy and why `p₁` never commits The environment plays against a TM ensuring strict serializability with two distinguished processes `p₁`, `p₂` and one t-variable `x`; all other processes only ever read `x` and never try to commit. This generalizes Strategies 1 and 2 of the paper (Algorithms 1 and 2 of the PODC version) so that it works under *any* scheduler, in particular a fair (round-robin) one: idle processes that are not "active" invoke filler reads of `x`. * `p₁` starts a transaction `T₁` by reading `x` (value `a`). It then keeps reading `x` until it is *ready*: some transaction of `p₂` that had not completed when `T₁` began has committed, the last such one wrote a value `d ≠ a` to `x`, and `p₂` has no pending commit request. Then `p₁` writes to `x` a value `b` that none of these transactions of `p₂` read first, and invokes `tryC`. * `p₂` starts each transaction by reading `x`. While `T₁` has read `a` and `p₁` is not ready, `p₂` writes `bump a ≠ a` to `x` and invokes `tryC`; otherwise it keeps reading `x`. The scripts depend on the current history only (via `info`). The invariant `Good` is preserved by every step allowed by the scripts and the TM, except a commit event for `p₁`; and such a commit would make the history not even serializable (`not_serializable_commit`): this is the paper's argument that "Algorithm 1 never terminates", for an arbitrary schedule.
