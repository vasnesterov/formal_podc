Liveness of Transactional Memory — PODC 2012

Victor Bushkov, Rachid Guerraoui, Michał Kapałka. On the liveness of transactional memory. PODC 2012. DOI 10.1145/2332432.2332435. Formalized version: the PODC preprint (EPFL Infoscience record 170486, dated 2011/12/5, 10 pages); numbering follows it. We also used the later write-up of the same results, V. Bushkov and R. Guerraoui, Liveness in Transactional Memory (Euro-TM book chapter, 20 pages), called "the chapter" below. Its Theorem 1, Lemma 1 and Theorem 2 are the PODC ones.

Verdict: 🛠️ Hard fix. Theorem 1, Lemma 1 and Theorem 2 hold in the model the paper intends, in all three fault-prone systems, and even with plain serializability, as the introduction claims. Read literally, the PODC formal framework makes them false, because a TM that never answers satisfies its definitions of opacity and local progress vacuously, so the framework had to be redesigned, and the proof of the crash-free case had a gap that a new schedule repairs. Theorem 3 (the automaton F_gp) and finite value sets were not checked.

Mistakes in the paper

🛠️ §2.2–2.3 and Definition 3 (the formal framework; Theorem 1, Lemma 1, Theorem 2 read literally)

Read literally, the PODC framework does not force a TM to answer, so the silent TM, which accepts one invocation per process and never responds, ensures local progress and opacity in every fault-prone system, contradicting Theorem 1 (PodcModel.silent_ensures).

Impact. In the model the paper intends (the chapter's step model) Theorem 1, Lemma 1 and Theorem 2 hold (theorem_1, lemma_1, theorem_2). The missing condition cannot be added as an assumption to the printed model, and always answering would exclude the blocking TMs the paper discusses (the global lock), so the framework has to change.

Details

In §2.2 a TM is an I/O automaton over invocation and response events. It has no internal steps and no fairness. §2.3 says a process crashes in an infinite history iff its projection is finite and non-empty, and liveness (Definition 3) only constrains the automaton's infinite histories. So a process whose invocation is never answered just counts as crashed. With finitely many processes the silent TM has no infinite history, so it ensures every TM-liveness property. All its transactions are live, so it is opaque. Hence it "ensures local progress and opacity in any fault-prone system". The proofs silently assume that every operation eventually returns ("p₁ invokes a read operation … and receives as a response v or A"). The intent is clear from §2.1 (classical asynchronous shared memory) and from the introduction (local progress as the analogue of wait-freedom).

Repair

The chapter fixes the model: executions consist of steps, a process crashes iff it takes finitely many steps, and a process that keeps taking steps in an operation that never returns is correct. We prove the results in that model (theorem_1, lemma_1, theorem_2).

🔧 §2.2 (well-formedness H|p_k ∈ Σ_k^∞)

§2.2 requires H|p_k ∈ Σ_k^∞ for every history of a TM, but histories are prefix-closed, so this forbids accepting any invocation.

Impact. One clause in one definition fixes it; in the step model well-formedness holds by construction.

Repair

The chapter allows a final pending invocation (Σ_k^* · Inv_k).

✏️ Slips

  • Theorem 1 says "for every fault-prone system" with no restriction, but it fails with one process, with no t-variable, or with a single value. In each case a TM that answers every operation immediately from a single store and never aborts is opaque and ensures local progress. We assume two processes, a t-variable x and infinitely many values.

  • §2.3 defines a crash as a finite non-empty projection. Then a process that never takes a step is correct and starving, and local progress fails trivially in every history where some process does not participate. The chapter drops "non-empty".

Typos

  • The proof of Lemma 1 writes pₙ for p₂ twice.

  • Theorem 2 says "serializable safety property". The definition in §5.1 is "strictly serializable", while the introduction claims "at least as strong as serializability". Both readings are true (theorem_2, theorem_2_serializable).

Proof gaps in true statements (2)Lemma 1, crash-free case · Algorithms 1 and 2

🛠️ Lemma 1, crash-free case (Algorithm 2)

Algorithm 2 runs one process at a time until its operation returns, but a crash-free system may block p₂'s operation until p₁ takes another step, so this schedule need not be admissible and the obstruction-freedom argument yields nothing.

Impact. Statement true; repaired with a round-robin schedule and a sturdier strategy (lemma1_crashFree, stratRun_ne_commit₁).

Details

The gap appears once the framework is fixed as in the first item; the PODC proof simply assumes that every invocation is answered, and under that stronger assumption Algorithm 2 works with two processes. Algorithm 2 is part of the proof, not of a stated claim. Algorithm 2 (Strategy 2 in the chapter) runs one process at a time until its operation returns, and only p₁ and p₂ ever take steps. A crash-free system admits only executions in which every process takes infinitely many steps. If p₂ runs alone, p₁ "crashes", the execution is not admissible, and the obstruction-freedom argument ("if the operation does not return, pₖ takes infinitely many steps and is correct") yields nothing. With three or more processes the idle processes crash as well. (The PODC definition of a crash as a non-empty finite projection hides this; see Slips.)

Repair

Our repair uses a round-robin schedule. It needs a sturdier environment strategy, since p₁ must now keep taking steps while p₂ works. Idle processes invoke filler reads of x. p₂ writes bump a, where a is the first read of p₁'s current transaction, and invokes tryC only while p₁ is not "ready". p₁ writes a value that no committed transaction of p₂ read first, and only once p₂'s last commit wrote a value ≠ a and p₂ has no pending commit request. This handles the "stale" commits that interleaving creates: a tryC of p₂ that is pending when p₁ is aborted can commit after p₁'s next transaction has begun. With this strategy p₁ can never commit, under any schedule. In a crash-free system the analysis is then short: if p₁ is parasitic, p₂ is correct but makes no progress, so it does not run alone; otherwise p₁ is correct and never commits. (lemma1_crashFree, stratRun_ne_commit₁)

🔧 Algorithms 1 and 2 (the conflict argument, Figures 8 and 11)

Each process writes v + 1 for its own read, and the argument that H_s is illegal assumes that p₁ and p₂ read the same v, which the text never justifies.

Impact. Statement true; a few lines close the step (conflict_seq).

Repair

For a committing transaction of p₁, safety of prefixes forces both reads to return the current committed value. The chapter uses cross values instead, which needs no such argument, and so do we.

The formalizationmodel, Lean names of the results, trust

Model.

  • Asynchronous shared memory with finitely many processes and read/write t-variables. Each step belongs to one process: an invocation by an idle process (the environment chooses it), or a step of the TM implementation for a process with a pending invocation. In such a step the implementation either returns a response or takes an internal step (tau). This is the chapter's step-based model.

  • The TM is an arbitrary nondeterministic agent. It sees the whole execution so far and chooses each step's outcome; its responses must fit the invocation. Every implementation from base objects induces such an agent, so the impossibility covers all implementations. The adversary is the environment, which picks the invocations and the schedule. There is no randomness.

  • Failures (PODC §2.3 with the chapter's crash notion). A process crashes iff it takes finitely many steps. It is parasitic iff it invokes infinitely often but is aborted and invokes tryC only finitely often. It is correct iff neither. It makes progress iff it commits infinitely often. The fault-prone systems are those of PODC: crashes and parasitic processes allowed, parasitic-free, or crash-free; a system constrains which executions the liveness property must hold for.

  • Liveness properties are predicates on executions. This is more general than the paper's sets of fair histories, which induce such predicates. "Runs alone" follows PODC: the process is the only correct one. Safety (strict serializability, serializability, opacity) is checked on the history of every finite execution, using sequences of transactions and the usual read/write semantics.

Sources of the proofs. Definitions and statements follow the PODC version: the three fault-prone systems, "runs alone", Definitions 4 and 5, strictly serializable safety properties. The execution model and the crash notion follow the chapter. The opacity conflict that stops p₁ from committing (PODC Figure 8, chapter Figure 6) is in both versions; we generalize it to stale commits and to serializability (conflict_seq). The parasitic-free case is the chapter's Strategy 1 (PODC Algorithm 1) with the chapter's argument that operations return. The crash-free case is new. The derivation of Theorem 2 from Lemma 1, and of Theorem 1 from Theorem 2, is the papers'.

About the chapter (not the paper judged here). The chapter changes the definition of "runs alone" to "all other processes crash" but keeps the PODC proof of Lemma 1. In the parasitic-prone case that proof concludes "p₂ runs alone" while p₁ is parasitic, not crashed. With the chapter's definition, Lemma 1 and Theorem 2 appear to be false in a crash-free, parasitic-prone system with two processes (sketch below, not checked in Lean). There, solo progress is vacuous, and a lock-based TM ensures strict serializability together with "if both processes are correct, both make progress". In that TM a transaction's first operation takes a global lock, operations of other processes are aborted, and an aborted process reserves the lock next. We did not formalize this counterexample. The chapter's crash-prone/parasitic-prone proofs also have the admissibility gap of the crash-free case (see Proof gaps).

Not formalized. Theorem 3 (§6: the automaton F_gp ensures opacity and global progress). Finite value sets with at least two elements: our strategy needs infinitely many values, and the paper's proof writes v + 1, so it assumes integer values. The counterexample to the chapter's version of Lemma 1. The examples (Figures 1–7, 14–16) and the remarks on existing TM implementations (§3.2).

Trust. No sorry. The listed theorems use only the axioms propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound. Non-vacuity: TMs exist, and the TM that never answers ensures opacity (silentTM_opaque); so theorem_1 does not hold merely because its hypotheses are unsatisfiable.
