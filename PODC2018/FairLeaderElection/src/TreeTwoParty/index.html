Mansour. *Fair Leader Election for Rational Agents in Asynchronous Rings and Networks.* PODC 2018 (arXiv:1805.04778v2), Appendix F. Two processors `A` (index `0`) and `B` (index `1`) connected by one FIFO link in each direction form the unidirectional ring of size `2` of `Cslib.UniRing`; in particular every processor has a single incoming link, so the result of an execution does not depend on the (fair) schedule (`Cslib.UniRing.terminates_iff`). `Agree a b o` says that both processors terminate with output `o`. A two-party protocol with a product input space `I_A × I_B` is described by the finite *sets* of processors `SA` (the strategies `S_A(i)`, `i ∈ I_A`) and `SB`. A processor *assures* a set of outputs `Q` if some deviation of it makes both processors terminate with a common output in `Q`, against every honest strategy of the other processor (Definition F.1; executions are schedule independent). **Lemma F.2** (generalized, `lemma_F2`): if every honest pair of strategies terminates with a common output in `Q₁ ∪ Q₂`, then `A` assures `Q₁` or `B` assures `Q₂`. The paper states this for outputs `{0, 1}` (`Q₁ = {0}`, `Q₂ = {1}` and the symmetric statement) and assumes in addition "a bounded amount of messages"; for finite input sets this bound follows from termination of the honest executions (`exists_bdd`), so it is not needed as a hypothesis. The proof is the paper's induction (on the maximal number of messages sent in an honest execution): * if some honest `A` and some honest `B` send nothing at initialization, both terminate immediately with a common output `o₀`, and both assure `o₀`; * otherwise one of them, say `A`, sends a first message `M` for every input; in the subprotocol `P_M` (`dropFirst`, `feed`) `B` has already received `M`, and the induction hypothesis either gives a strategy of `A` assuring `Q₁` after sending `M` (`prepend`), or for every `M` a strategy of `B` assuring `Q₂` in `P_M`, combined into "wait for the
