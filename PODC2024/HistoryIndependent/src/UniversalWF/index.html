The proof is a potential argument. Fix a process `p` with a pending state-changing operation. Its *phase* is * `0`: before announcing (line 4); * `1`: announced, not yet applied (`announce[p] ∈ O`, `head ≠ ⟨_, ⟨_, p⟩⟩`); * `2`: applied, not yet cleared (`announce[p] ∈ O`, `head = ⟨_, ⟨_, p⟩⟩`); * `3`: cleared (`announce[p] ∈ R`) or after the loop. In phase 1 the priority-based helping is measured by `Φ = Σ_r φ_r`, where `φ_r` is the distance from `priority_r` to `p` (mod `n`), or `n` if `r` is *stale*: it is about to apply its own operation although `priority_r = p` because it read `announce[p]` before `p` announced. Every transition `A → B` (a successful `SC` in line 14) that does not apply `p`'s operation decreases `Φ`, and every transition `B → A` decreases the mode bit. The global part `G` of the potential never increases; it strictly decreases at every write to `head` that removes `p` from the context of `head`. The local part `pl` decreases at every step of `p` that does not decrease `G`. Hence `μ = 32 G + pl` never increases and strictly decreases at every step of `p`. (The paper's argument "after at most `n` successful SCs by `p_j`, `priority_j = π(op)`" overlooks the stale reads: a process may need `n + 1` of them.
