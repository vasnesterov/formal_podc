Differences between the formalization and the paper

Rendered from differencies.md of Strong Linearizability using Primitives with Consensus Number 2 — PODC 2024.

Attiya, Castañeda, Enea. Strong Linearizability using Primitives with Consensus Number 2. PODC 2024 (arXiv 2402.13618v1, the same text as the proceedings version).

Classification: false statement / false only in a degenerate case / proof gap (statement true or repairable) / under-specified definition / typo / modelling choice.

Errors and gaps

  1. Theorem 10 / Algorithm 2 (set from test&set): false as proved. Algorithm 2 is not strongly linearizable, whether or not taken_new is incremented. We use three processes. p₀ runs put(1) and p₁ runs put(2); both do their fetch&increment, so Max = 3. q runs take(). It finishes a whole first iteration with Items[1] = Items[2] = ⊥, reads Max = 3 again and reads Items[1] = ⊥. Then put(1) writes Items[1] and returns. Call this prefix π. In the extension B, q reads Items[2] = ⊥ and returns EMPTY (its double collect succeeds). Since put(1) completed in π and 1 is never taken, q's take must precede put(1) in the linearization. So it already belongs to h(π), with response EMPTY. In the extension A, put(2) writes Items[2] and q takes 2. This contradicts the entry of h(π). The paper's proof places a take → EMPTY at "its last step that reads Max", which depends on the future. At that point both slots are still ⊥; but the proof's claim that when a take returns empty every slot is ⊥ or taken is false here: Items[1] = 1 and TS[1] = 0 at the return. The flaw does not depend on the taken test: the take sees no non-⊥ slot, so even a classic double collect comparing the read Items values returns EMPTY here. Theorem 10 (a lock-free strongly-linearizable set exists from test&set) is therefore unproved, and Figure 1's arrow to "set" is unsupported. We did not study whether another algorithm works. False statement (as proved). (SetCE.alg2_not_stronglyLinearizable)

  2. Section 5: "k-out-of-order queues are k-ordering": false for the given sequences. The paper takes prop_i = enq(i), dec_i = deq, and S_α = the inputs of the first k enqueues of α. When α has fewer than k enqueues, the k oldest items include items enqueued in α', which depend on i. Example with n = 3, k = 2, α = enq(1): process i ∈ {0, 2} may enqueue i and dequeue i. Together with process 1 (which dequeues 1), this forces |S_α| ≥ 3, for every choice of d. Wrong witness (proof error); the claim itself is true. It is repaired by prop_i = enq(i)^k (KOrd.ooo_not_kOrderingW; repair: KOrd.ooo_kOrderingI). With the paper's sequences, Theorem 19 is not established for k-out-of-order queues with k ≥ 2. With the repaired sequences the reduction applies (modulo item 3, which the repair survives).

  3. Lemma 12, proof gap: linearizations of other processes' pending operations. In the proof, f(F_i) = α · α' · β_i with invs(β_i) = dec_i. But F_i extends E_i*|A, in which other processes may have pending operations of prop_j. A strong linearization function may linearize them during p_i's solo simulation of dec_i (helping). So f(F_i) = α · α' · γ, where γ interleaves dec_i with up to one operation of prop_j per other process j. Definition 11 does not cover such γ. The fix is to strengthen Definition 11 accordingly (KOrderingI).

    • Queues (and k-out-of-order queues with the repaired sequences) satisfy the strengthened definition (KOrd.queue_kOrderingI, KOrd.ooo_kOrderingI). So Theorem 17 for queues stands, modulo the unformalized parts of Lemma 12.

    • Stacks do not, with the paper's sequences (prop_i = push(i), dec_i = pop^{n+1}), for any d (n = 3). Process 0 sees OK · 0 · 1 · 2 · ε in two scenarios. In the first, α = push(1), α' = push(0), and the pending push(2) is linearized after the stack has been emptied. In the second, α = push(2) and α' = push(1) · push(0). Then d would have to equal both 1 and 2 (KOrd.stack_not_kOrderingI). The scenario is realizable (informal argument, not formalized). Take a lock-free strongly-linearizable stack from CAS and registers in which a pop that finds the stack empty first applies an announced pending push (linearization points at successful CASes). Algorithm B of Lemma 12 then violates agreement. Hence the impossibility for stacks (Theorem 17 for stacks, its relaxations, and the remark in §6 that a stack has no wait-free SL implementation from CN-2 primitives) is not established by the paper's argument. Under Definition 11 as stated, the stack example itself is correct (KOrd.stack_kOrdering): the definition is too weak for the lemma. Proof gap; for stacks, the claimed consequence is unproved.

  4. Definition 11 vs. its examples: the chosen process ℓ. Definition 11 requires invs((α·α')|ℓ) = prop_ℓ (ℓ completed its proposal sequence). The stuttering queue/stack examples only establish invs(α|ℓ) ≠ ε, as the paper itself notes ("and possibly ≠ prop_ℓ"). Validity in Lemma 12 needs only that ℓ has started, since M[ℓ] is written before ℓ's first operation. KOrderingI/KOrderingW use "ℓ has started". Under-specified definition.

  5. Definition 11, invocation condition. invs(α) ⊆ ⋃ₓ invs(prop_x) is a set inclusion. Read literally, it allows histories in which process j executes another process's proposal, and then even queues are not 1-ordering. The intended condition, used in the proof (and by us), is that every process executes a prefix of its own prop_j. Under-specified definition.

  6. Theorem 3 (simple types from snapshots): proof sketch, not formalized. The forward simulation relates C₁ to every topological sort of lingraph(G₁*). The step case needs sorts for C₂ that extend a given sort for C₁. But lingraph adds dominance edges greedily along a topological sort of the whole graph, so new nodes can change the edges among old nodes. This is not addressed ("It is easy to see that…"). The correctness of the responses is deferred to [7, 27]. We neither confirm nor refute the proof; the result is Ovens–Woelfel's (PODC 2019). Proof sketch.

Typos and slips

  • Algorithm 2's caption says "From test&set to fetch&increment"; it is the set algorithm.

  • Algorithm 2: taken_new is reset to 0 and never incremented, so the taken test is vacuous. Both readings are modelled (countTaken).

  • Theorem 9: "the state of the implemented object is the maximum index i such that M[i] is 0" should be the minimum (equivalently, the number of set entries; they form a prefix).

  • Proof of Theorem 17: "A is a lock-free strongly-linearizable queue" should be "… implementation of the 1-agreement (1-ordering) object". The paper uses both "1-agreement" and "1-ordering" for the same notion.

  • Definition 11 asks for a set S_α "with k process indexes", but the examples use 1 ≤ |S_α| ≤ k; we use |S_α| ≤ k.

  • Proof of Theorem 19: only linearizability and wait-freedom of Theorem 5's implementation are needed to preserve B's (safety) correctness, not strong linearizability (remark, not an error).

Modelling choices

  • Model. CSLib's asynchronous shared memory: deterministic processes, atomic base objects, executions are label lists (inv / ret / internal step tau). Each internal step makes exactly one base-object access. Local computation is folded into the transitions, and returning is a separate step. There are no explicit crashes: a crashed process simply takes no more steps, and strong linearizability is a property of finite executions.

  • Strong linearizability (Cslib/Computability/Distributed/SharedMemory/StrongLin.lean) is the Golab–Higham–Woelfel definition: a linearization function on finite executions with α ≤ β ⇒ h α ≤ h β. A label list determines the execution, so h may depend on internal steps. For nondeterministic specifications (the set, k-out-of-order queues), linearizations carry the responses of their operations, pending ones included (IsNLinearization). For deterministic objects we use CSLib's IsLinearization; it implies the nondeterministic notion (StronglyLinearizable.nStronglyLinearizable).

  • Theorems 1, 2. Values are natural numbers. WriteMax returns 0 as acknowledgement, and update returns the zero view. Process i may only update component i (allowed). The snapshot's fetch&add register holds an integer, since the adjustment posAdj − negAdj may be negative; the max register uses a natural-number register. ReadMax's decoding counts the set bits of each process (unary). Processes are Fin n.

  • Theorem 5. ts is an ordinary atomic test&set (accessible by all processes) and state is a Boolean register; false stands for 0. Processes form an arbitrary finite type.

  • Theorem 6. The base objects are atomic. curr is a max register initialized to 1, and the TS[v] (v : ℕ) are atomic readable test&set objects, initially 0. TS[0] exists in the model but is never accessed. reset returns false as acknowledgement. We prove the paper's linearization exactly: at e_v, first the pending test&set/read operations on TS[v], then the resetter, then the other resets that read v.

  • Theorem 9. M is an array of atomic readable test&set objects (ℕ-indexed, initially 0). Operations are linearized when they obtain 0. Lock-freedom (finitely many processes) is proved with one potential that strictly decreases at every invocation and internal step while no operation responds; this differs from the paper's informal argument but proves the same property.

  • Composition with Theorems 1 and 5 is not formalized: Corollaries 7 and 8, and the last step of Theorem 9 ("the theorem follows due to this implementation and Theorem 5").

  • Algorithm 2 counterexample. Items are natural numbers, ⊥ = none, three processes, and Max is initialized to 1. The implemented object's ObjSpec is a dummy used only for the Op/Resp types; the specification is the nondeterministic setSpec.

  • Definition 11. Sequential histories are lists (process, operation, response) legal for an NSpec. S_α has at most k elements. Three versions: KOrdering (as stated, with the per-process reading of item 5), KOrderingI (what Lemma 12's proof needs), and the common weakening KOrderingW.

Not formalized

  • Theorem 3 / Theorem 4 (simple types), and Corollaries 7, 8: CSLib has no composition theorem for (strongly) linearizable implementations.

  • Lemma 12, Corollary 14, Lemma 16, Theorems 17, 19 and Corollaries 15, 18. The reduction needs one algorithm simulating another over readable base objects, plus execution surgery (Claim 13); this infrastructure does not exist yet. Corollaries 15/18 are cited impossibility results (Herlihy; Herlihy–Rajsbaum). We formalized Definition 11 and its examples, which is where the errors are (items 2–5).
