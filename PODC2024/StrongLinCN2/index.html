Strong Linearizability using Primitives with Consensus Number 2 — PODC 2024

Hagit Attiya, Armando Castañeda, Constantin Enea. Strong Linearizability using Primitives with Consensus Number 2. PODC 2024. DOI 10.1145/3662158.3662790. Formalized version: arXiv 2402.13618 v1, whose text and numbering are the same as the proceedings version. Copy of the paper: paper.pdf.

DOI · arXiv · PDF · All differences · Lean source · GitHub

Verdict: ⚠️ Weakened. All positive constructions we checked hold: the max register and snapshot from fetch&add (Theorems 1, 2), readable test&set (Theorem 5), multi-shot test&set (Theorem 6) and fetch&increment (Theorem 9), each strongly linearizable and wait-free or lock-free as claimed. But Algorithm 2, the set behind Theorem 10, is not strongly linearizable, and a gap in the proof of Lemma 12 leaves the impossibility for stacks unestablished. The impossibility for queues was not checked: we formalized Definition 11 and its examples, not the reduction.

Mistakes in the paper

❌ Theorem 10 (Algorithm 2, set from test&set)

Algorithm 2 is not strongly linearizable: with three processes running put(1), put(2) and take(), a prefix exists after which the take must already be linearized with response EMPTY in one extension but returns 2 in another (SetCE.alg2_not_stronglyLinearizable).

Impact. Theorem 10, a lock-free SL set from test&set, is unproved; we did not study whether another algorithm works. All other positive constructions (Theorems 1, 2, 5, 6, 9) hold.

Details

Both puts do their fetch&increment (Max = 3). The take scans Items[1..2] = ⊥, reads Max = 3 again and reads Items[1] = ⊥. Then put(1) writes Items[1] and completes; call this prefix π.

  • In one extension the take reads Items[2] = ⊥ and returns EMPTY (double collect unchanged). Since put(1) completed and 1 is never taken, the take must be linearized before put(1). So it already belongs to h(π), with response EMPTY.

  • In another extension put(2) writes Items[2], and the take reads it and returns 2.

So no prefix-closed linearization function exists. The paper linearizes an EMPTY at "its last read of Max", a point that depends on the future. (At that point both slots are still ⊥; but its claim that when the take returns EMPTY every slot is ⊥ or taken fails here, with Items[1] = 1 and TS[1] = 0.) This holds whether or not the never-incremented taken_new is read as a counter of failed test&sets.

❌ Lemma 12 and Theorem 17 for stacks (helping in the reduction)

Lemma 12's proof ignores that the strong linearization function f may linearize pending proposal operations of other processes during p_i's solo simulation, and stacks with the paper's sequences fail the stronger definition this needs (KOrd.stack_not_kOrderingI), so the stack case of Theorem 17 is unestablished.

Impact. Unestablished, not refuted: the impossibility results for stacks and their relaxations, and the remark in §6 about stacks, are not established by the paper. Queues satisfy the stronger definition (KOrd.queue_kOrderingI), so the queue case is unaffected.

Details

The proof writes f(F_i) = α·α'·β_i with β_i made only of p_i's decision operations. But Definition 11 must allow pending proposal operations of others interleaved in β_i (our KOrderingI). Stacks with the paper's sequences (push(i) / pop^{n+1}) do not satisfy this, for any d (KOrd.stack_not_kOrderingI, n = 3). Process 0 observes OK·0·1·2·ε both when α = push(1) and a pending push(2) is linearized after the stack has been emptied, and when α = push(2), α' = push(1)·push(0). A lock-free SL stack (from CAS) whose pop on an empty stack first applies an announced push realizes this, and then the reduction B violates agreement (informal argument, not formalized).

Under Definition 11 as literally stated, the stack example is correct (KOrd.stack_kOrdering): the definition is simply too weak for the lemma.

✏️ Slips

  • Definition 11 requires the chosen process ℓ to have completed prop_ℓ, while the stuttering examples only give that ℓ started. Evident intent: "started" is also all that validity needs. We use the repaired reading.

  • The condition invs(α) ⊆ ⋃ invs(prop_x) of Definition 11 must be read per process: the proof uses the per-process reading (each process executes a prefix of its own prop_j). We use this reading.

  • Section 4.2 and the proof of Theorem 9 say "maximum index i with M[i] = 0"; it means the minimum.

Typos

  • Algorithm 2's caption ("From test&set to fetch&increment").

  • taken_new is never incremented.

  • Theorem 17's proof says "queue" where it means "1-agreement object"; "1-agreement" and "1-ordering" are used interchangeably.

  • |S_α| = k vs ≤ k.

Proof gaps in true statements (1)Section 5

🔧 Section 5 ("k-out-of-order queues are k-ordering")

The witness prop_i = enq(i) fails: with n = 3, k = 2, α = enq(1), processes 0, 1, 2 can decide 0, 1, 2 respectively, so no S_α of size 2 works, for any d (KOrd.ooo_not_kOrderingW).

Impact. Wrong witness; the statement itself is true with other sequences.

Details

The processes' own enqueues in α' are among the 2 oldest items.

Repair

prop_i = enq(i)^k, which works even for the strengthened definition (KOrd.ooo_kOrderingI).

The formalizationmodel, Lean names of the results, trust

Model.

  • CSLib's asynchronous shared memory with deterministic processes and atomic base objects (Impl). Executions are label lists, and every internal step is one base-object access.

  • Crashes are modelled as processes that stop taking steps. Wait-freedom and lock-freedom are CSLib's WaitFree / LockFree on infinite executions.

  • Strong linearizability (Cslib/Computability/Distributed/SharedMemory/StrongLin.lean) follows Golab–Higham–Woelfel: a linearization function on finite executions with α ≤ β ⇒ h α ≤ h β.

    • Deterministic objects use CSLib's IsLinearization.

    • Nondeterministic objects (set, relaxed queues) use NSpec / IsNLinearization, where linearizations carry responses. The deterministic notion implies the nondeterministic one.

  • Positive results are proved by a forward simulation into the atomic object (Impl.LinSim ⇒ stronglyLinearizable_of_linSim), following the paper's linearization points, including helping at Theorem 5's first write and Theorem 6's e_v.

  • Theorems 1 and 2 use the generic OneAccess theorem: single-access implementations with an abstraction relation are SL and wait-free.

  • Base objects are atomic. The compositions with Theorems 1 and 5 (Corollaries 7, 8; the last step of Theorems 9, 10, 19) are not formalized, since CSLib has no composition theorem.

  • Processes:

    • Fin n for Theorems 1 and 2;

    • any finite type for Theorems 5, 6, 9 (needed to list helped operations and for lock-freedom);

    • three processes for the counterexample.

Not formalized.

  • Theorems 3, 4 (simple types; the proof is a sketch) and Corollaries 7, 8 (need a composition theorem). Theorem 3's forward simulation relates a configuration to every topological sort of lingraph(G*). The step case needs sorts that extend earlier ones, and lingraph's greedy edge insertion does not obviously provide them; we neither confirm nor refute the sketch.

  • Lemma 12 with Claim 13, Corollary 14, Lemma 16, Theorems 17, 19, and the cited Corollaries 15, 18. The reduction needs one algorithm simulating another over readable base objects, plus execution surgery. We formalized Definition 11 and the examples instead, which is where the errors are.

  • That Theorem 9's implementation is not wait-free.

Trust. No sorry. All theorems in the table use only the axioms propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound.

  • The counterexample's executions are checked by kernel evaluation of a computable copy of Impl.step, proven equal to it.

  • Non-vacuity: all positive theorems are about concrete implementations, and their allowed predicate is True, except for the single-writer snapshot. queue_kOrdering and stack_kOrdering show that the definitions of k-ordering are satisfiable.
